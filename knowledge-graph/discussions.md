## 1. Direction mismatch: the cheap filter may be inspecting the wrong side of the connection — OPEN, circling back

The stated reason for adding pKVM isolation to network traffic was closing the asymmetry with files: `PacketUtils`/`TcpRelayManager` parse network-controlled bytes unisolated in the host process today, the same risk class as the EXIF parser's real fuzzing-found heap overflow.

But the packet sampled for the VM check is captured "at the exact moment the destination is first flagged" — and a destination is flagged the moment `NetMonitorService`'s tun-read loop first sees traffic *to* it, i.e. the outbound packet an app on this device is sending out, generated by this device's own OS network stack. That's not attacker-controlled data. The genuinely hostile bytes — a malicious server's *response* — are parsed by `TcpRelayManager`'s reply-pump later, after the connection is live, and never reach this mechanism as currently specified, because they don't exist yet at flag-time.

**Not resolved.** If the goal is real, the sample likely needs to be inbound response data, captured after the connection is established, not the initiating outbound packet. Revisit before `noxos-payload` builds a guest-side check against data that was never the actual threat.

**Built, session 17 (`noxos-app` commit `bcfda8e`) — promoted to `PROJECT.md`'s addendum and `noxos-app/TASKS.md` session 17.** The sample now includes the first inbound reply alongside the outbound packet (see item 3's resolution below for the mechanism) — the guest-side check, once built, can and should look at the destination-controlled bytes now, not just the outbound one. Doesn't fully close the "is the check itself real" concern in item 4 below (that's still `noxos-payload`'s open design call), but the data it would need to be real is now actually captured.

## 2. Session-scoped forgetting — RESOLVED, by design, not a bug

Clarified by the user: session-scoping is the intended *default* tier, not a gap. The ACL itself is the permanence layer — it's local, persistent storage, and a user who wants a verdict to stick adds/promotes it there directly. "Whatever a session decides stays ephemeral until you make it permanent" is the actual two-tier model, working as intended.

**Residual note worth keeping in mind, not a bug**: a destination nobody ever manually curates into the ACL gets zero cross-session benefit from being resolved — it's re-flagged, re-VM-checked, and (if it escalates) re-asked of the AI/classifier every single session, indefinitely. Purely passive users get no accumulating protection; only actively-curated entries do. Fine as a design choice, just worth being deliberate that this is the real, permanent shape of it rather than something to "eventually improve."

## 3. In-memory packet buffer has no failure/recovery path — RESOLVED (direction agreed 2026-09-13, not built yet)

Concrete scenario: `NetworkSampleVmDispatcher` needs `NetMonitorService.pendingPacketSamples` (in-process RAM only) to actually run its check. If the VPN service process dies or restarts between "destination flagged" and "dispatcher picks it up" (battery-saver killing a background service, the user toggling monitoring off/on, a crash), the buffered packet is gone. The ACL row survives (it's a real DB row: `FLAGGED`, `cheapFilterChecked = false`) but nothing re-triggers capturing a fresh sample, nothing notices the row is stranded, and nothing surfaces this state anywhere (not the audit trail, not the ACL screen). That destination silently never resolves — not allowed, not blocked, not escalated — forever, with zero visibility into why.

**Needs a decision**, not just documentation: options include (a) persist the sample somewhere durable (a small table, not just RAM) until dispatched, (b) a staleness sweep that re-flags/re-samples a `FLAGGED` row that's sat unchecked past some window, or (c) explicitly accept the loss and make it visible (e.g. a distinct ACL state like "sample lost, needs a fresh connection to retry") rather than an indistinguishable-from-normal `FLAGGED`.

**Proposed resolution (2026-09-13), agreed direction — real code change, not built yet:**
- Rely on natural client retry behavior instead of building explicit staleness-recovery machinery. Concretely: `pendingPacketSamples` stops being "one packet, captured once, at flag time" and becomes **a per-destination buffer that keeps appending every packet seen for that destination for as long as it's unresolved** (`FLAGGED`, `cheapFilterChecked = false`). No explicit "retry detection" needed — a retried connection just generates more packets, which land in the same still-open buffer; whenever the dispatcher's next poll runs, it grabs whatever's accumulated (one packet or several) and dispatches that as the sample.
- **If never retried**: the row just stays `FLAGGED` with an empty/never-flushed buffer, indefinitely. Accepted as fine — the connection was already relayed live at first contact (existing cost-gating behavior), so a permanently-unresolved row isn't blocking anything, just permanently uncategorized. Same shape of accepted tradeoff as item 2's "passive users get no cross-session carryover."
- **Concurrency — decided 2026-09-13: mutex lock**, not a lock-free/accepted-race approach. A plain `Mutex`-guarded buffer (or `synchronized`) between the capture-loop coroutine (appending) and the dispatcher (reading + clearing) — traffic volume here is far too low for lock contention to ever be a real cost, so this removes the race for free rather than budgeting for a rare lost packet.
- **Reuses item 7's structure**: this accumulating per-destination buffer is the same data structure item 7's batching proposal needs (batch N destinations' samples into one VM call) — build it once, it serves both.

**Built, session 17 (`noxos-app` commit `bcfda8e`) — two honest deviations from the proposal above, flagged not hidden:**
- **Concurrency: `CopyOnWriteArrayList` per destination, not an explicit `Mutex`.** Functionally equivalent for this use (its own `add()` is internally synchronized, safe for the capture-loop coroutine and both relay paths to append from concurrently, safe for the dispatcher to iterate/remove) — just a different, arguably simpler mechanism than the literal "mutex-guarded buffer" this item proposed. No lock object, no `synchronized` block, same "no lost writes" guarantee.
- **Capped at 2 samples per destination, not unbounded until dispatched.** The proposal above argued a cap wasn't needed ("traffic volume here is far too low for lock contention to ever be a real cost") — true for contention, but a chatty still-unresolved flow could otherwise buffer many packets in the 30s window before the dispatcher's next poll, which is an unnecessary memory/privacy cost for a mechanism that only needs "the outbound trigger plus one inbound sample" to do its job. `appendPendingSample()` silently stops appending past 2 entries per destination rather than growing further — the retry-driven recovery behavior (a retried connection just adds to whatever's already buffered) still works exactly as designed, it just doesn't accumulate every single packet of a long-lived, still-uncategorized flow.
- Batching multiple *destinations* into one VM call (item 7) is unaffected either way — still not built, still a separate, larger design question.

**Correction, session 19, from a peer review — the "Built, session 17" claim above was wrong on the retry premise itself.** The accumulating buffer (`CopyOnWriteArrayList`) really was built and works — but the *capture gate* that would need to fire again for a retry to land anything in it never got touched: `NetMonitorService`'s capture loop only appends to `pendingPacketSamples` inside `if (verdict == null && ...)`, and once a destination is `FLAGGED`, `aclCache[dstIpStr]` is never `null` again. A retried connection to an already-flagged, still-unresolved destination generates real packets, but none of them re-enter that block — the buffer never actually receives a second sample from a retry, contrary to what "the retry-driven recovery behavior... still works exactly as designed" claimed. In practice this made the design fall back to the *original* accepted-loss shape (a stranded entry just sits forever) — except worse, because `nextCheapFilterBatch()`'s oldest-first-no-cutoff query meant a stranded entry also permanently occupies a batch slot, silently starving every destination flagged after it once enough accumulate. **Real fix, session 19 (`noxos-app` commit `00da1ce`)**: a 5-minute staleness cutoff on `nextCheapFilterBatch()` — option (c) from the original 2026-09-13 discussion ("explicitly accept the loss... rather than an indistinguishable-from-normal FLAGGED", partially — it's not yet *visible* anywhere, just no longer *starving*), simpler than fixing the capture gate and doesn't depend on retry timing at all. Full detail in `noxos-app/TASKS.md` session 19. Lesson for future "Built" notes in this file: verify the actual gate/call site the design depends on, not just that the data structure it was supposed to feed exists and behaves correctly in isolation.

## 4. Wire contract locked ahead of the actual security semantics — acknowledged deliberate, still needs a real spec

User's framing: the contract was locked *specifically* so `noxos-payload` (a different agent) isn't blocked waiting on this repo — reasonable, and consistent with why this session exists (laying ground framework/API contracts across agents). Not disputing that sequencing.

Still true and still needs resolving before `noxos-payload` starts implementing: `noxos-payload/TASKS.md` currently says the actual check "is this repo's design call... the exact heuristics aren't specified anywhere." Without a concrete spec, there's real risk of a check that's technically real but provides no security value (e.g. "IP version is 4 and checksum is valid" — true of literally every packet that reaches the tun, since the kernel already guarantees that) while still paying a VM-boot cost per destination.

**Candidate starting spec, for review before handing to `noxos-payload` (not decided, just a starting draft):**
- Declared IP total-length field vs. actual received byte count (mismatch = flag).
- For TCP: SYN sample specifically — window size / flags sanity (e.g. reserved bits set, contradictory flag combos like SYN+FIN together).
- For UDP: declared UDP length field vs. actual datagram size.
- Whatever check is chosen should be validated against point 1 above first — inspecting the outbound SYN for internal consistency has limited value if the real threat is inbound response data.

## 5. On-device classifier: real signal today is close to a free existing function — OPEN

`noxos-app` can currently supply exactly two features per destination: `dst_port` (usable) and `protocol` (not yet numeric-encoded, so effectively unusable by the interpreter as shipped). Every other feature the model expects (the other ~37 UNSW-NB15 columns) falls back to `feature_defaults`, which were deliberately computed as "normal traffic" medians/modes. So the model's actual, real-world decision today tracks `dst_port` almost exclusively.

The codebase already has `NetworkPriority.classify(port)` — a free, already-shipped, zero-dependency function doing exactly that (standard vs. non-standard port → LOW/HIGH priority).

**Open question, not resolved**: is it worth wiring the on-device classifier into the live pipeline before real per-flow feature capture (volume/frequency counters, deliberately not built due to the per-packet-DB-write cost concern already flagged in `noxos-inference/TASKS.md`) exists? As specified today, wiring it in would likely add JSON-asset size, tree-walk cost, and export-pipeline maintenance for a decision that's currently indistinguishable from the one-liner already in production.

**Sequencing agreed 2026-09-13**: model choice (item 8) has to be finalized *before* the real feature list can be decided — what packet-derived data actually needs capturing/persisting follows from what the chosen model needs, not the other way around. No feature-capture work should start until item 8 is resolved.

## 6. XGBoost `dump_model` → custom JSON transform: real edge-case risk, not "mechanical" — OPEN

`noxos-inference/TASKS.md` describes the export as "a small recursive rewrite... a mechanical transform, not a retrain." One specific risk that framing undersells: XGBoost's real dump format identifies a missing-value branch by a **child node ID** (`missing: <id>`), and that ID has to be matched against the node's actual `yes`/`no` children to determine whether "missing" means "go left" or "go right" in the simplified `default_left` boolean this interpreter expects — it is not guaranteed to always be positionally `children[0]`.

Get this wrong and nothing crashes or errors — the interpreter just silently gives the wrong answer specifically on the missing-value path, which (per item 5 above) is the dominant path for nearly every real prediction right now, since almost every feature is currently "missing" and defaulted. This is exactly the failure mode you don't want in a security gate: wrong and silent, not wrong and loud.

**Needs a real test once an actual export exists**: verify the interpreter against a real `booster.dump_model()` output from the actual trained model, not just the hand-written synthetic two-tree fixture currently used in `OnDeviceNetworkClassifierTest` — that fixture proves the interpreter's own logic is self-consistent, not that it correctly reads what XGBoost actually produces.

**Recommended next step (2026-09-13): resolve item 8 first, because it changes whether this risk exists at all.**
1. If item 8 lands on a **scikit-learn-based tree ensemble** (Random Forest, or any sklearn `DecisionTreeClassifier`-based model): this entire risk category disappears. sklearn trees are plain threshold-only binary splits (`feature`/`threshold`/`children_left`/`children_right`) with no native missing-value/default-branch semantics — missing features get imputed (filled with the same `feature_defaults` infrastructure already built) *before* the tree ever sees them, not handled inside a `yes`/`no`/`missing` node the way XGBoost's dump format does. This is a concrete, separate reason to prefer Random Forest here, beyond accuracy or phone-weight concerns (see item 8) — it removes a whole class of silent-wrong-answer bug, not just a training-quality one.
2. If XGBoost is kept anyway: don't implement the transform as a positional/mechanical rewrite. Match `yes`/`no` child IDs explicitly to determine the missing-value branch, and write a real parity test — run the actual trained Python model and the Kotlin interpreter against identical feature vectors, including deliberately-missing ones, and assert they agree — before shipping.
3. Either way: hold off building the actual export script (`training/export_ondevice_model.py`) until item 8 is decided. Building and testing an XGBoost-specific transform is wasted work if the model changes.

## 7. VM-boot cost per newly-flagged destination vs. Pillar 1 (Lightweight) — proposal: batching

**Problem restated**: normal browsing can hit many genuinely-new destinations quickly (CDNs, ad/tracking domains, font hosts, API endpoints — often a dozen-plus per page load), and combined with item 2's per-session reset, this could mean a real burst of one-shot VM boots at the start of every session, in tension with the "keep the common case cheap" framing and the project's own Lightweight pillar.

**User's proposed fix: batch processing.** Instead of one VM per newly-flagged destination, batch N unique destinations' packet samples (e.g. up to some count, or up to some short time window) into a single VM invocation. Tradeoff, explicitly accepted: the *first* batch of a browsing burst pays a real, noticeable lag waiting for the batch to fill/flush and the VM to process it; every destination in that batch, once classified, resolves and its subsequent traffic flows at full speed with no further delay. This mirrors the existing "first contact always relays live regardless of ACL state" behavior — nothing about live relay changes, only how quickly a batch of pending verdicts gets resolved.

**Not yet designed, real open questions**:
- Batch trigger: fixed count, fixed time window, or whichever fires first?
- What does the payload's response shape look like for a batch (one verdict per sample, keyed how)?
- Does a batch mix samples from unrelated destinations in one VM invocation change anything about the "audit trail per destination" story, or is it still one ACL write per destination out of one VM call?

## 8. On-device model choice: XGBoost vs. Random Forest — OPEN, needs a decision with `noxos-inference`

**Input from the user's friend**: XGBoost run with too few boosting iterations (1-2) gives poor results; needs roughly 16-32+ to be any good; suggested considering Random Forest instead, on the concern that XGBoost might be "too heavy for a phone."

**My technical read on this, for the record — flagging a nuance, not overriding the suggestion:**
- The part that's correct: a gradient-boosted model genuinely does underfit badly with too few boosting rounds — that's real and worth checking. **Fact-check needed, not yet done**: nobody has recorded what `training/train_network_model.py`'s actual `n_estimators` is. The already-reported 95.75% accuracy / 0.969 F1 suggests it's probably not egregiously underfit, but this should be confirmed directly rather than assumed either way, same spirit as the earlier `AclEntity` fact-check.
- The part I'd push back on gently: "XGBoost is heavy for a phone" isn't quite the right framing for *this* architecture specifically, because the phone never runs XGBoost at all — it runs a plain hand-rolled tree-walk interpreter over an exported JSON (that's the whole point of the on-device design, avoiding a real ML runtime). Walking a tree is walking a tree regardless of whether XGBoost or Random Forest produced it; the phone-side cost is a function of total tree count × depth, not which training algorithm was used. So the real phone-cost question is "how many trees, how deep" — not "which library."
- If a move to Random Forest happens anyway (for accuracy/robustness reasons, which is a legitimate reason on its own): it's not a drop-in swap on the export side. Random Forest is a bagging ensemble (majority vote / averaged probability across independent trees), not a boosted one (summed margin → sigmoid) — the interpreter's actual math and the export JSON's top-level fields (`base_score`, margin-summing) documented in `noxos-inference/TASKS.md` would need real changes, not just a retrain with the same shape.
- Also worth weighing before deciding: Random Forests often need *more* trees than a well-tuned boosted model to reach comparable accuracy (commonly 100+ for stability) — which could mean a *larger* on-device asset and *more* tree-walks per prediction, not fewer. Not disqualifying, just a real tradeoff against the "lighter for phone" motivation, worth checking empirically rather than assuming either direction.

**Not decided.** Needs a joint call with whoever's driving `noxos-inference`'s training work — this changes what gets exported, not just how `noxos-app` reads it.

## 10. On-device vs. server model scope, decided 2026-09-14

**Two branches exist in `noxos-inference`** (`feature/on-device-xgboost-classifier`, `feature/ml-server-architecture`, both currently at the same commit as `main`, no divergent work yet) — **decided these are genuinely two different models, not one artifact served two ways**:
- **On-device**: the lean model, scoped to exactly what `noxos-app` can supply without new capture work (see below). Ships as the exported tree JSON, evaluated by `OnDeviceNetworkClassifier`.
- **Server (`feature/ml-server-architecture`)**: deliberately **richer**, not just a hosted copy of the on-device model. Scope not fully nailed down yet beyond "genuinely more than what the phone sends" — candidates are an external threat-intel/reputation lookup, cross-device aggregated signal, or compute-heavy work that only makes sense off-phone (e.g. real SHAP-based explainability generation, which is legitimately too heavy to run per-request on a phone). Not designed in detail yet — flagged here so nobody builds it as a same-model reskin by default.

## 9. The real feature contract, checked directly against code and the trained model (2026-09-13) — the dataset/deployment mismatch is worse than assumed

Verified directly (real CSV header, real `train_network_model.py`, real `service/app.py`, the real trained artifact's `feature_importances_`) rather than reasoned about secondhand. Findings, most important first:

**(a) Today, nothing `noxos-app` sends reaches the model at all — a real bug, not just feature starvation.** `service/app.py`'s `encode_request()` looks up each of the model's 42 real column names by exact string match in the JSON payload (`payload.get(col, default)`). `noxos-app` sends `ip`/`priority`/`reason`/`first_flagged_epoch_millis`/`dst_port`/`protocol`. None of those match a real column name — there is **no port field anywhere in the training data at all** (checked the raw CSV header directly), and `protocol` isn't `proto`. Even fixing the name wouldn't be enough: `proto`'s learned vocabulary is lowercase (`'tcp'`, `'udp'`, ... 133 values total, checked against the real model artifact), and `noxos-app` sends `"TCP"`/`"UDP"`/`"OTHER"` — a case/vocabulary mismatch that would still resolve to "unknown category" every time. **Net effect: every real request today gets the exact same normal-defaulted prediction, regardless of destination.** This is a real, fixable contract bug between `noxos-app` and `noxos-inference`, separate from the deeper feature-availability question below.

**(b) The real training feature set: 42 columns (39 numeric + 3 categorical), not 39 as `training/FEATURES.md` currently states** (off by the 3 categorical columns — worth a doc fix whenever this area gets touched again). Categorical vocabularies, from the real trained artifact:
- `proto`: 133 values (general network-protocol names — `tcp`, `udp`, `icmp`, `ospf`, `arp`, `sctp`, ...)
- `service`: 13 values (`http`, `dns`, `ftp`, `ssh`, `ssl`, `smtp`, ... plus `-` for unknown)
- `state`: 7 values (`CON`, `FIN`, `INT`, `REQ`, `RST`, `ECO`, `PAR`)

**(c) `service` is unusable as a real signal, and this isn't a Warden-side gap — it's baked into the dataset itself.** Checked the real distribution: `service = '-'` (unknown) in **53.7% of the entire training set**. More than half the original data never had a real service label. This is very likely why `service` ranked low in feature importance (0.0068, rank 14/42) even for the model that has full access to every column — it's genuinely low-information, not just hard to compute on-device.

**(d) The single dominant feature (`sttl`, 74.7% of the model's total `feature_importances_`) may not transfer to Warden's deployment at all.** `sttl` is the *connection-initiator's* IP TTL. In Warden's setup the initiator is always the phone — every outbound packet carries whatever fixed default Android's network stack sets, which won't meaningfully vary by destination. The reason `sttl` is so predictive in the original UNSW-NB15 dataset is very plausibly a testbed artifact: that dataset was captured between distinct attacker/victim lab machines that likely ran different OS images with different default TTLs — a documented failure mode in older NIDS benchmark datasets, where a model learns "which lab machine generated this" rather than "is this malicious." **Not proven — I can't test this without real phone traffic — but a serious, specific reason to distrust the 95.75% headline accuracy for this deployment specifically**, not a vague "small feature set = worse" concern.
- `dttl` (the *destination's* TTL — the one that could plausibly vary by which real server you're talking to) scored **0.0000** importance in the actual trained model. Whether that's a real null result or another testbed artifact of the same kind isn't known from here.

## What to do next (decided 2026-09-13) — sequencing, not a final architecture call

Four options were weighed, ranked cheapest-to-most-thorough:
1. **Cheap diagnostic**: retrain on the existing UNSW-NB15 data but restricted to only the columns Warden can realistically supply (`proto` properly encoded, `sttl`, `swin`/`dwin`, buffer-accumulated packet-count/duration proxies) and check the real accuracy/F1 on the same held-out split. Data's already downloaded, the training script already exists — near-zero cost, turns "feels like it won't work" into a real number.
2. **Reconsider the signal, not just the feature count**: parse the TLS ClientHello's SNI extension (the destination hostname, sent in cleartext even under TLS 1.3, readable from the very first outbound packet of a TLS connection) as the primary on-device signal instead of flow statistics — domain reputation / DGA-heuristic style classification, structurally a better fit for what a phone-side, payload-blind, mostly-TLS vantage point can actually see than porting a flow-stats model down ever will be. `PROJECT.md` already mentions SNI in passing as a future threat-intel seed source; this would elevate it to a primary signal, not just a seed list input.

## 13. Server model (Tier 2 / `feature/ml-server-architecture`) scope, decided 2026-09-14

Following on from item 9's server/on-device split decision — this is what "genuinely richer" concretely means, decided in a dedicated brainstorm. **Honest scale note up front**: this is a solo project, not a fleet of devices — cross-device aggregated intelligence (the usual reason a cloud security model beats an on-device one) isn't realistically available here, and claiming it would be the same kind of inflated framing the CARE-X pivot already rejected ("don't fake a multi-institution setup with one dataset"). Scoped to what's actually real at this project's scale instead.

**Decided components:**

1. **Persistent incident history feeds the bigger model, as an input feature.** The on-device ACL is deliberately session-scoped (forgets AI verdicts every session, by design — item 2). The server has no reason to forget — "has this destination ever been flagged before, across every session this device has run" is a real feature only Tier 2 can hold, independent of any fleet-scale story. This alone justifies Tier 2 existing.
2. **External threat-intel source: AbuseIPDB, confirmed via its real docs (not assumed).** Two endpoints, both used, different roles:
   - `blacklist` — bulk list of reported IPs (up to 10,000), free tier: **5 pulls/day**. Pull once daily, cache locally, use as a fast first-pass check. Real caveat: only includes IPs above a 25% confidence floor — coarser than a live check, fine for a first pass only.
   - `check` — single-IP lookup with a real confidence score, free tier: **1,000/day**. Used live for actual Tier-2 escalations, which need precision the cached blacklist doesn't have.
   - **Fallback, not co-primary**: a static free blocklist (FireHOL level1/level2, or Spamhaus DROP) for whenever AbuseIPDB itself is unreachable — same graceful-degradation pattern as `service/clamav_client.py`'s "uncertain, not a crash" convention.
3. **Enrichment is a lookup/cache, not something that "learns" — clarifying a real conflation from the brainstorm.** AbuseIPDB doesn't self-learn from being queried; it's read-only reference data someone else maintains. The *model that consumes* the enrichment result is what retrains periodically — don't confuse "refresh the cache on a schedule" with "the enrichment step is learning."
4. **Checked and rejected: using the training dataset's own `srcip`/`dstip` as a real-world IP-reputation source.** UNSW-NB15's IP addresses are synthetic, lab-internal addresses generated by the IXIA PerfectStorm tool inside ACCS's cyber range — they have no relationship to real internet addresses Warden will ever see. Mechanically extractable, but would mean memorizing lab addressing, not learning anything that transfers. Real threat-intel has to come from a real-world source (AbuseIPDB) — the dataset itself can't supply this feature no matter how it's sliced.
5. **Human-feedback batch retraining, periodic, not online learning** (same conclusion as item 9's "self-learning" clarification, now with real safeguards specified). A scheduled batch job pulls human-confirmed labels (a user's manual ACL override is exactly a confirmed label) and retrains on a cadence. **Data-cleaning safeguards, specified now so they're not an afterthought later**: cap how much influence any single device/session can have on one retrain cycle; don't let a new label silently override a very high-confidence prior verdict without a second look; version and log every retrain so a bad one can be rolled back. Real design work for whenever this pipeline gets built — not needed today, just specified so it isn't skipped later.
6. **SHAP explainability: server-side (Tier 2) only, not on-device.** Real SHAP (even the efficient tree-specific variant) costs meaningfully more than a plain prediction; Tier 1 runs far more often than Tier 2 by design, so doubling its cost undercuts the reason Tier 1 exists at all. **On-device explainability uses a cheaper rule-based check instead** — "which of the 10 locked features was furthest from its typical/expected value" — a real, decided substitute, not a placeholder.

## 14. Full XAI architecture, resolved 2026-09-14 — two pipelines, never touching each other at runtime

Clarified directly with the user's friend (the one who originally suggested "BETA" — confirmed via web search to be a real technique, **BETA = "Black Box Explanations through Transparent Approximations"**, Lakkaraju/Bach/Leskovec, arXiv:1707.01154 — two-level decision sets: an outer region descriptor + inner per-region decision rules). Full picture, confirmed correct by the friend after a round of clarifying back-and-forth:

**Evaluation-phase (dev-facing, dataset-only, offline) — PDP and ICE:**
- Run against the fixed public UNSW-NB15 dataset (or a sample of it), **never against real Warden traffic, never on a phone, never automatically on a schedule.** One-time (or per-retrain) sanity check: "does this trained model's behavior make causal sense, or did it learn something spurious from the data" — the same instinct that caught `sttl` being a source-side/lab artifact earlier.
- **PDP**: cheap, one averaged curve per feature — the first-pass triage tool. Already run for real against the locked 10-feature model (see item 11's numbers) — flagged `smean` (genuinely bumpy, non-smooth) and `handshake_latency_millis` (suspiciously narrow 0–1.4ms range, possibly another lab-network artifact) as worth a closer look.
- **ICE** (centered): targeted follow-up specifically on features PDP already flagged, not run indiscriminately on all ten. Reveals whether a bumpy PDP average is real-and-consistent behavior or hidden heterogeneity/interaction effects the average was masking. Not yet run — next concrete step on `smean`/`handshake_latency_millis` specifically.
- **Real diagnostic sequence for future retrains**: train → PDP (cheap triage) → ICE only on whatever PDP flags → decide whether to trust the feature or investigate further. Not a fixed schedule — a step in the human-driven retrain/validation process.

**Deployment-phase (user-facing, real-instance, runtime) — SHAP (Tier 2) and BETA (Tier 1):**
- Explains one real flagged destination's actual verdict, using the already-validated model — completely separate code path from PDP/ICE, which never runs here.
- **SHAP** (server, Tier 2) — exact, per-instance, `TreeExplainer`. Affordable here since Tier 2 only processes the small residue Tier 1 already flagged.
- **BETA** (on-device, Tier 1) — replaces the earlier ad-hoc "furthest from typical value" placeholder. Fit once, offline, at export time (same pipeline as the tree export, one more artifact); on-device evaluation is just "which rule matched," as cheap as the tree walk itself.
- **Open, real, not-yet-decided refinement**: whether to layer LIME's sparse (K-LASSO) local linear coefficients on top of BETA's matched region, for a bit more per-feature granularity within the "why" — designed as a concept (fit offline alongside BETA, ship a small sparse-weight artifact, no live perturbation on-device) but not committed to; BETA's plain rule text alone may already be enough for what a phone notification needs. Decide once there's a concrete UI mockup to judge against, not before.
- **On-device UI plan, not yet built**: a compact per-instance bar chart (3-5 bars, magnitude + direction) in Warden's existing `AuditDetailScreen`, fed by whichever attribution numbers (SHAP server-side, or BETA/sparse-LIME on-device) are already being computed for the text explanation — a rendering task, not new ML work.

**RAM note, since it came up in the same conversation**: none of this — model, BETA rules, sparse weights, the small per-instance chart — costs meaningful memory on-device. The multi-million-row dataset and the PDP/ICE sample never ship to the phone at all; only the tiny exported model file does. The real memory considerations on-device are the VPN relay's buffers and the bounded caches (2-sample cap, 5-minute staleness cutoff, audit-log retention) already built for unrelated reasons — see `PROJECT.md`/`noxos-app/TASKS.md` for those.

## 15. The headline F1=0.9724 is real but inflated — real number on genuinely novel data is F1≈0.88, recall≈0.85

Checked directly, prompted by a real "did you leak the data" question — not classic leakage, not classic overfitting, but a third, related problem that matters just as much for trusting the number.

**Ruled out, checked directly**: (a) train/test split mechanics are clean — categorical vocabulary (`proto`) is learned from train only and reused (not refit) on test, no label information crosses the boundary. (b) Classic overfitting is not present — train-set F1 (0.9740) vs. test-set F1 (0.9724) is a gap of only 0.0016, the opposite signature of overfitting (which would show train much higher than test).

**What's actually happening**: **54.68% of test rows have an exact, byte-for-byte duplicate feature-row already in the training set** (47% of the entire 2.28M-row dataset is duplicate feature-rows). This is a direct, mechanical consequence of reducing every connection down to only 10 coarse numbers — two genuinely different real connections (different IPs, different timestamps, different everything in the richer original data) can easily collapse to the exact same `(proto, dst_port, byte counts, duration, ...)` tuple once reduced to just these 10 fields. Nothing about this is a bug in the split code; it's an honest cost of the feature-reduction decision that hadn't been accounted for in how the headline number got reported.

**Real, honest number — model performance restricted to ONLY the 206,689 test rows with no exact duplicate in training:**
```
                Full test set (incl. duplicates)   Genuinely novel rows only
Precision       0.9792                              0.9167
Recall          0.9657                              0.8519
F1              0.9724                              0.8831
```
Recall (the metric that matters most for a security tool — are real attacks actually caught) drops from 96.6% to 85.2% on genuinely novel-looking traffic. **The 0.9724 number already documented throughout `PROJECT.md`/`noxos-inference/TASKS.md` should be read as an optimistic upper bound, not the number to expect on real, never-before-seen Warden traffic** — F1≈0.88 / recall≈0.85 is the more honest estimate.

**Real follow-up, not yet done**: check whether the earlier "42-feature ceiling" comparison (the one used to justify "only 1.35 points behind full-feature" in the feature-selection work) has the same duplicate-collision problem. More columns should mean far fewer collisions — if so, the *true* gap between the 10-feature on-device model and the full model, measured fairly on novel-only data for both, is likely larger than 1.35 points. Don't trust that comparison's exact margin until this is checked the same way.

**Not a reason to abandon the current model or feature set** — the relative comparisons between feature-set sizes throughout this whole exploration used the same (equally duplicate-inflated) methodology, so they likely stayed consistent with each other even if each one's absolute number was optimistic. But the *absolute* F1/recall numbers reported anywhere in this project for the on-device model should be corrected to reflect this, and real-world validation (once Phase 1c ships and real traffic flows through the cascade) matters even more than these dataset-internal numbers suggested.

**Decided fix, 2026-09-14, dispatched to `noxos-inference`: switch to a group-based train/test split, not a row-based one.** `train_test_split` splits at the row level, which lets identical feature-patterns land on both sides — the actual root cause of the 92,702-shared-pattern problem above. Fix: build a group key per row from the exact 10 training features, then use `sklearn.model_selection.GroupShuffleSplit(test_size=0.2, random_state=42)` (or `GroupKFold`) keyed on that group, so every row sharing a pattern goes entirely to train or entirely to test, never both. After splitting: verify zero shared patterns remain (confirm, don't assume), and check the resulting class balance hasn't skewed badly (group-based splitting can distort balance if a few patterns are very frequent — flag it if so, don't silently accept it). Retrain on the corrected split, and **the resulting number supersedes 0.9724 everywhere it's documented** — not a footnote alongside it, a replacement. Full prompt text handed to the `noxos-inference` agent, not repeated here.

## 16. Group-split fix verified independently, 2026-09-14 — real number is F1≈0.98, not 0.88 or 0.9724, and here's why those don't contradict

Plain `GroupShuffleSplit` (no stratification) hit exactly the failure mode item 15's fix prompt warned about: it fixed the leak but skewed class balance badly (own test run: train attack rate 7.06% vs. test 23.43%, >3x apart) — not a trustworthy number, flagged instead of reported. Separately, the `noxos-inference` ground agent hit the same problem and switched to `StratifiedGroupKFold` on its own initiative, got a suspiciously clean-looking F1=0.9858 — user correctly got suspicious ("these values are wrong... just because you told it it should be 88") and paused the agent before trusting it, since nothing had been committed yet to check against.

**Verified independently from scratch** (own script, own grouping — label column explicitly asserted out of the group key, the one way this number could be faked): `StratifiedGroupKFold(n_splits=5)`, one fold held out as test. Result: **zero group overlap** (checked, not assumed), **class balance matches exactly on both sides** (11.09%/11.09%, same as the full dataset), 236,618 distinct test groups averaging 1.9 rows each (broad, representative sample — not a few duplicated groups dominating). **acc=0.9965 prec=0.9797 rec=0.9893 f1=0.9845** — within noise of the ground agent's independently-obtained 0.9858. Two separate implementations agree; this is a real, structurally-verified number.

**Reconciling 0.88 vs. ~0.98 — they measure different things, not the same thing twice:** the 0.8831 number (item 15) came from filtering the *original leaky split's* test set down to rows whose pattern never appeared in that same split's training set. Random row-sampling makes that filtered bucket skew hard toward **rare, low-frequency patterns** — a common pattern is nearly certain to land in an 80% random train sample by chance; a rare one might not. So 0.88 is an accidental hardest-case/rare-pattern-only metric, not a representative one. `StratifiedGroupKFold` instead builds a properly representative ~20% sample across *all* distinct patterns (common and rare alike) and retrains fresh on the complement — most held-out patterns are structurally close to things seen often in training, so the model does well on them, legitimately.

**Both numbers are honest and leak-free; they answer different questions**: 0.88 ≈ worst-case floor on the hardest sliver of genuinely novel traffic; ~0.98 ≈ realistic average-case on a representative sample of never-verbatim-seen traffic. For a deployed Tier 1 classifier, ~0.98 is the right headline number — 0.88 stays documented as a worst-case floor, not discarded.

Do not treat ~0.98 as final/shipped until a real commit lands; re-verify the committed code's group-key construction directly (not from printed/narrated output) before superseding 0.9724 in `TASKS.md`.
